Station 01 · ≈5 min

The puzzle: a wall that flows

Think about a soap bubble. You can push a wet finger straight through it and pull it back out, and the bubble survives. Poke it with a dry finger and it pops instantly.

Your cell membranes do something similar. A researcher can push a fine glass needle into a cell to inject DNA, pull it out, and the hole closes on its own. A white blood cell can squeeze through a gap smaller than itself. Yet the same membrane keeps ions like Na+ out so reliably that your neurons depend on it.

So the membrane is a barrier that is also flexible, self-healing, and constantly moving. Hold on to that tension. The rest of the lesson explains it.

Station 02 · ≈10 min

Phospholipids: molecules with split personalities

The main building block of the membrane is the phospholipid. It has two very different ends:

Hydrophilic head. A glycerol backbone attached to a negatively charged phosphate group (often with another small polar group). It is polar, so it forms hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions with water.

Hydrophobic tails. Two long fatty acid chains made almost entirely of C–H bonds. They are nonpolar and cannot interact favorably with water.

A molecule with both a polar and a nonpolar region is amphipathic. When you drop amphipathic molecules into water, the water molecules push the tails together (the hydrophobic effect). The most stable arrangement is a bilayer: heads face water on both sides, tails hide in the middle. No enzyme or energy input builds it. It assembles itself.

One phospholipid, up close

Saturated vs. unsaturated tails. A saturated fatty acid has only single C–C bonds, so it is straight and packs tightly against its neighbors. An unsaturated fatty acid has at least one cis C=C double bond, which puts a kink in the chain. Kinks keep tails from packing tightly. You will use this idea in Station 05.

Station 03 · ≈10 min

How we know: evidence that built the model

Nobody can see a membrane with a light microscope. The model came from decades of indirect evidence, and it changed when new evidence did not fit. Read the timeline.

  1. 1925

    Gorter & Grendel extracted lipids from red blood cells and spread them in a single layer on water. The layer covered about twice the surface area of the cells. Conclusion: the membrane is a lipid bilayer.

  2. 1935

    Davson & Danielli proposed a "sandwich": a bilayer coated on both faces by flat sheets of protein. It explained why membranes were more permeable to water than pure lipid.

  3. 1960s

    Problems appeared. Membrane proteins turned out to be amphipathic and varied, and their hydrophobic regions would be exposed to water in the sandwich. Membranes from different cells had different thicknesses and protein content.

  4. 1960s–70s

    Freeze-fracture electron microscopy split membranes down the middle between the two layers. The inner faces were studded with bumps: proteins embedded in the bilayer, not lying on top.

  5. 1970

    Frye & Edidin fused a mouse cell with a human cell and tracked their membrane proteins with two colors of fluorescent antibody. Try their experiment below.

  6. 1972

    Singer & Nicolson proposed the fluid mosaic model: proteins embedded in and drifting through a fluid lipid bilayer. It is still the accepted model today.

Fluidity lab: tune the membrane

A membrane works only in a middle zone. Too rigid (gel-like) and transport proteins cannot change shape, and the membrane can crack. Too fluid and it becomes leaky and loses its barrier. Cells adjust their membranes to stay in the functional zone.

  • A fish lives in 2 °C Arctic water. Set temperature to 5 °C or lower and get the membrane back to functional.
  • A desert lizard basks at 45 °C. Set temperature to 42 °C or higher and keep it functional.
  • Show cholesterol helping at both extremes: functional at ≤5 °C and at ≥42 °C with unsaturation left at 50%.

Why cholesterol is a "buffer." At warm temperatures, its rigid ring structure restrains phospholipid movement, lowering fluidity. At cold temperatures, it wedges between phospholipids and keeps them from packing tightly, which keeps the membrane from solidifying.

Who gets in? Selective permeability

The hydrophobic core of the bilayer is the gatekeeper. Small, nonpolar molecules dissolve into it and slip across. Ions and large polar molecules cannot get through the oily middle on their own; they need a channel or carrier protein.

Where the bubble model breaks down

A model is useful because it is simpler than the real thing, but you should know its limits:

  • A soap film is "inside-out" compared with a cell membrane. Its polar heads face a thin layer of water in the middle, and its nonpolar tails face the air on both sides.
  • Bubbles have no proteins, cholesterol, or carbohydrates, so they cannot model transport, recognition, or signaling.
  • A cell membrane sits in water on both sides; a bubble sits in air.
